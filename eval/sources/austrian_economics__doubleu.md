## 1. Introduction

Austrian economics is a branch of economics, with its inception in the 19th century, emphasizing individual choice and free markets. The concept originated in the work of Carl Menger and has been refined by Ludwig von Mises, Murray Rothbard, and others. Austrian Economics offers a perspective on economic behaviour and policy distinct from other branches.

We will set out and discuss tenets of this philosophy before reviewing critiques from other economic schools. We will also make comparisons with the ever-popular collectivist Keynesian economics and have a look at the real-time application of Austrian principles in Argentina by Javier Milei — an economics Professor, author of several books, and Austrian economist. We will also propose how Austrian economics principles might improve the prospects for the UK and USA.

## 2. Key Features of Austrian Economics and Its Philosophy

Austrian economics is defined as the science of individual choice, focusing on human action through deductive logic, termed praxeology. Founded by Carl Menger in 1871 in his pioneering Principles of Economics, it emphasizes the subjective nature of economic value and the spontaneous order of markets. Menger describes his theory as an economic framework based on concepts such as individual human action, subjective value, marginal utility, and the spontaneous coordination of markets through individual choices. Menger suggests these are capable of functioning in an economy without reliance on centralized planning or objective measures of value.

The Austrian school traces its intellectual roots to 15th century scholars, who identified economic concepts such as supply and demand and advocated for establishment and protection of property rights and free trade.

Key foundational principles of Austrian economics include:

**Subjective Value and Marginal Utility:** Value is determined by individual preferences, with the concept of 'marginal utility' suggesting that the value of additional units decreases as supply increases. Menger's work in 1871 formalized this concept, solving the diamond-water paradox by showing value depends on subjective needs rather than objective properties. This paradox questions why diamonds, which have little practical use, are valued more highly than water, which is essential for survival. Menger resolves this by emphasizing that value is not inherent in goods but depends on individuals' subjective judgments of their utility in specific circumstances; the value of a good is determined by its marginal utility — the satisfaction provided by the last unit of that good available to the individual. Water is abundant in most contexts, so its marginal utility is low; the additional unit of water adds little to one's well-being. Diamonds, being scarce, have a higher marginal utility because each additional diamond satisfies a more significant want.

**Market Process:** Prices emerge from individual interactions in an entrepreneurial process, as noted by Richard Cantillon in 1730. This process coordinates economic activity without central planning. Cantillon's price theory revolves around the interplay of supply, demand, and what he calls "intrinsic value." In Essai Chapter 10 he writes:

> "The intrinsic value of a thing is the measure of the quantity of land and of labour entering into its production, having regard to the fertility or produce of the land and to the quality of the labour."

**Time Preference and Interest:** Eugen Böhm-Bawerk in 1884 highlighted that interest rates reflect a preference for present over future satisfaction. This is due to human tendency to value goods available immediately more than these same goods being available in the future. This preference arises because people generally prefer immediate satisfaction of needs or wants over delayed satisfaction. He argued that the concept of interest exists because individuals discount the value of future goods compared to present goods.

**Money and Business Cycles:** Ludwig von Mises' 1912 Theory of Money and Credit argued that money originates in markets and that business cycles result from artificial credit expansion, distorting investment signals. Mises suggested 'business cycles' arise when the money supply is artificially expanded through credit expansion, especially when central banks lower interest rates below the "natural rate" — i.e. a free-market rate set by participants. This artificial credit expansion misleads entrepreneurs, distorting price signals and leading to malinvestment. Von Mises warns:

> "The inevitable outcome of credit expansion is the breakdown of the boom and the appearance of the crisis."

**Laissez-Faire and Property Rights:** Austrians advocate minimal government intervention, emphasizing property rights and free contracts, as seen in the Late Scholastics' opposition to taxes and regulations. A comprehensive quote by Ayn Rand in The Nature of Government explains why:

> "The only proper purpose of a government is to protect man's rights, which means: to protect him from physical violence... The only proper functions of a government are: the police, to protect you from criminals; the army, to protect you from foreign invaders; and the courts, to protect your property and contracts from breach or fraud by others, to settle disputes by rational rules, according to objective law. But a government that initiates the employment of force against men who have not used force against others — such a government is not a protector but a violator, and it becomes the most dangerous enemy of man's rights."

**Critique of Socialism:** Mises' 1921 essay and 1923 book Socialism argued that socialism inevitably fails due to the absence of price signals for resource allocation:

> "Without economic calculation, there can be no economy. Hence, in a socialist state wherein the pursuit of economic calculation is impossible, there can be — in our sense of the term — no economy whatsoever."

**Say's Law:** Jean Baptiste Say's principle, defended by Henry Hazlitt, suggests that in a free market, supply creates its own demand, preventing sustained overproduction. From Say's 1802 work:

> "A product is no sooner created than it, from that instant, affords a market for other products to the full extent of its own value."

Ludwig von Mises in Socialism: An Economic and Sociological Analysis encapsulated these ideas:

> "All rational action is, in the first-place, individual action. Only the individual thinks. Only the individual reasons. Only the individual acts."

He further emphasized the market's role:

> "The market system is the basis of our civilization. Its only alternative is the Führer principle."

## 3. Detailed Discussion of Austrian Economics in Understanding the Real Economy

The Austrian Economics model emphasizes the efficiency of free markets and minimal government intervention, believing that the market self-corrects and that government actions can distort economic signals. In contrast, Keynesian Economics relies on government intervention, such as macro level fiscal and monetary stimulus, to stabilize aggregate demand during economic downturns. This fundamental difference shapes their views on economic cycles, with Austrians seeing them as natural and necessary for correcting imbalances, while Keynesians view recessions as failures of aggregate demand requiring active policy responses.

Austrians also prefer 'sound' money, such as gold or commodity-backed currencies, and are critical of fiat currencies which lead to inevitable central bank manipulations that lead to inflation and consequent malinvestment. Keynesians, on the other hand, support fiat currencies and see controlled inflation as a tool to stimulate growth. Methodologically, Austrian Economics is based on deductive reasoning with a focus on individual action and 'subjective value'; which contrasts with the approach used in Keynesian Economics, which focuses on aggregates. Mises noted:

> "Government manipulation of money and credit in the banking system throws savings and investment out of balance, resulting in misdirected investment projects that are eventually found to be unsustainable."

Fritz Machlup also commented: "monetary factors cause the cycle but real phenomena constitute it," emphasizing the school's focus on underlying economic realities.

Austrian economics is often considered superior in explaining how economies function in reality compared to the Keynesian 'Aggregate' model. The Austrian concept emphasises individual choice as being central to economic value. This contrasts with Keynesian thought, from John Maynard Keynes' 1936 "General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money," which focused on government managing aggregate demand in order to deal with peaks and troughs in the economic cycles.

Austrians, influenced by the free market ideals of the 19th century, argue for minimal government involvement based on the belief that markets naturally self-regulate. Keynesians, after the Great Depression, advocated for active fiscal and monetary intervention to stimulate demand, a view that dominated post-World War II economic policy until the 1970s, when stagflation challenged its effectiveness.

Several economists have acknowledged the influence and contributions of Austrian economics. Alan Greenspan, in hearings before U.S. Congress in 2000, stated:

> "The founders of the Austrian school 'reached far into the future from when most of them practiced and have had a profound and, in my judgment, probably an irreversible effect on how most mainstream economists think in this country.'"

James M. Buchanan also identified with Austrian ideas, noting in the Austrian Economics Newsletter, "I have no objections to being called an Austrian."

Austrian economics is often considered better at explaining how economies function due to its emphasis on individual decision-making, subjective value, and the self-regulating nature of free markets. It critiques Keynesian interventions as potentially distorting economic signals and leading to malinvestment, advocating instead for natural corrections through economic cycles.

## 4. Critiquing Austrian Economics

Despite its contributions, Austrian economics faces significant criticism, particularly from mainstream economists. They argue that its strong belief in market efficiency overlooks real-world market failures, such as the 2008 financial crisis driven by subprime mortgages and securitization.

Several Western European economies run on high taxes and government spending, yet maintain social freedoms and robust welfare systems. This is used as an example to challenge Austrian economists' claims that government intervention inherently restricts liberty. The practicality of controlling the money supply, as Austrians advocate, is questioned, with historical examples like the UK's 1920s gold standard causing deflation and unemployment. Austrians would argue that this misery of extended deflation and unemployment was precisely because the free market was not allowed to deleverage and quickly recover. Austrian models are often viewed to be excessively subjective, vague, and lacking in empirical rigor. Keynesian economists argue that government intervention is essential to stabilize economies during recessions, contrary to Austrian reliance on market self-correction.

Economist Bryan Caplan provides a detailed critique, arguing that Austrian attempts to rebuild economics on different foundations fail due to misunderstandings of neoclassical economics. He criticizes what he considers to be several inconsistencies in Austrian theory, such as Rothbard's dismissal of neoclassical 'utility' functions. Caplan also criticises the overuse by Austrians of Mises' 'economic calculation' argument against socialism, ignoring other issues like incentives. The Austrian Business Cycle Theory (ABCT) is faulted for assuming irrational entrepreneurial expectations and making inconsistent predictions about employment and output during economic busts. Furthermore, the Austrian focus on individual choice and the rejection of mathematics and econometrics isolates Austrian economics and limits its contributions to mainstream economics.

## 5. Javier Milei and Argentina's Economic Performance

Javier Milei, an economics professor who followed the Austrian School, was elected president of Argentina in December 2023. He immediately implemented policies rooted in Austrian economics, focusing on reducing government size, and promoting free markets. His administration inherited an economy with 211.4% inflation and 41.7% poverty in 2023. Key outcomes in the Argentinian economy after two years are:

- **Inflation Reduction:** Monthly inflation dropped from 25.5% in December 2023 to 2.7% by October 2024, with an annual rate of 193%. This aligns with Austrian emphasis on controlling monetary expansion.
- **Fiscal Surplus:** Achieved the first budget surplus in 12 years through austerity measures like subsidy cuts, layoffs, and wage freezes.
- **Currency Stabilization:** The black-market dollar gap with the official rate shrank by 44%, and a tax amnesty brought $19 billion into banks.
- **Economic Challenges:** Consumer spending fell 20%, poverty rose to 52.9%, and unemployment increased, with an estimated 200,000 construction jobs lost due to suspended public works.
- **Financial Indicators:** The country-risk index reached its lowest in five years, and bonds rallied, but Argentina still owes $44 billion to the IMF.

Milei's policies reflect his belief in minimal state intervention:

> "The state is a coercive machine to steal resources from the private sector."
>
> "Free Market Capitalism is not only a possible system to end world poverty, but also the only morally desired system to achieve it."

Despite economic improvements, the social costs highlight the challenges of rapid Austrian-style reforms. Austrians would argue that Argentina has lived beyond its means on debt and international loans, rather than real productivity, for many decades.

## 6. Implementing Austrian Economics (Example of the UK and USA)

Bringing in Austrian economic practice in the UK and USA would involve significant policy shifts in favour of free markets and reduced government intervention. General requirements would include:

- **Monetary Policy Reform:** Reducing central bank influence, possibly adopting market-driven interest rates or a gold standard.
- **Fiscal Policy:** Cutting government spending and taxes to minimize intervention.
- **Deregulation:** Removing barriers to competition in industries like finance, energy, and healthcare.
- **Privatization:** Selling state-owned enterprises to enhance efficiency.
- **Free Trade:** Minimizing tariffs to promote global trade.
- **Labor Market Flexibility:** Reducing regulations to allow flexible employment.

### UK Context

- **Post-Brexit Opportunities:** Deregulating financial and other sectors to attract investment, aligning with Austrian emphasis on competition.
- **Bank of England Reform:** Limiting monetary interventions to stabilize prices without distorting markets.
- **Tax and Spending Cuts:** Reducing high taxes and public spending, particularly on welfare, to encourage private sector growth.
- **Challenges:** Resistance from a public accustomed to a strong welfare state and political opposition to deregulation.

### USA Context

- **Federal Reserve Reform:** Advocating for its abolition or significant reduction in its role, promoting free banking.
- **Tax Cuts:** Further reducing corporate and income taxes, building on policies like the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act.
- **Debt Reduction:** The unsustainable and growing debt of the USA, from deficit spending, must be checked and reversed. This will be painful as total debt is over $250,000 per US taxpayer.
- **Deregulation:** Streamlining regulations in healthcare and energy to boost competition.
- **Privatization:** Privatizing services like the postal service or Amtrak.
- **Challenges:** Political polarization and reliance on federal programs like Social Security and Medicare.

## 7. Conclusion

Austrian economics offers a conceptual framework for understanding economic behaviour through individual choice and market processes. Its emphasis on freedom and scepticism of government intervention contrasts with mainstream approaches, particularly Keynesian economics. While Javier Milei's application in Argentina shows both successes and challenges, implementing Austrian principles in the UK and USA would require overcoming significant political and social barriers. The Austrian School's principles are invaluable for fostering debate on economic policy and how limited the role of government should be in modern economies.

Von Mises in Socialism (1922) and Human Action (1949), contended that government intervention, such as price controls or nationalization, disrupts market signals and creates inefficiencies. This cycle of ever-increasing government intervention, he warned, erodes individual liberty and concentrates control in the state, eventually resembling communist or collectivist systems where central planning dominates.

Mises believed socialism, even in milder forms, inherently suppresses the price mechanism which is essential for coordinating economic activity. Without prices reflecting supply and demand, resources are misallocated, leading to shortages and economic decline. Given a choice between market failure that costs a few extra cents on one's groceries or fuel; or government failure that costs one's freedoms and even their lives — the least worse option is flawed free markets every time. The Austrian School is fundamentally about maintaining this individual choice and economic freedom. As Hayek said:

> "The principle that the end of government is the good of mankind, and that the individual has a right to pursue his own happiness, is the foundation of a free society." (The Constitution of Liberty, 1960)
